                            Image-based Asset Pricing

                      in Commodity Futures Markets∗


                Yuecheng Jia†              Hongyu Zhang‡                Jiangyu Zhao§

                                      This version: July 9, 2025

                                                Abstract

    We introduce a deep visualization (DV) framework that turns conventional commodity
data into images and extracts predictive signals via convolutional feature learning. Specif-
ically, we encode futures price trajectories and the futures surface as images, then derive
four deep-visualization (DV) predictors, carry (bsDV ), basis momentum (bmDV ), momen-
tum (momDV ), and skewness (skDV ), each of which consistently outperforms its traditional
formula-based counterpart in return predictability. By forming long–short portfolios in the
top (bottom) quartile of each DV predictor, we build an image-based four-factor model that
delivers significant alpha and better explains the cross-section of commodity returns than ex-
isting benchmarks. Further evidence shows that the explanatory power of these image-based
factors is strongly linked to macroeconomic uncertainty and geopolitical risk. Our find-
ings reveal that transforming conventional financial data into images and relying solely on
image-derived features suffices to construct a sophisticated asset pricing model at least in
commodity markets, pioneering the paradigm of image-based asset pricing.

1     Introduction

The quest to explain cross-sectional variation in commodity futures returns has produced a
vast “factor zoo,” encompassing momentum (e.g., Bakshi, Gao, and Rossi (2019)), carry
(Koijen, Moskowitz, Pedersen, and Vrugt (2018)), basis momentum (Boons and Prado
(2019)), and higher-order moments such as skewness (Fernandez-Perez, Frijns, Fuertes, and
Miffre (2018)) among others. However, fixed-formulaic and point-in-time measures, based
on current returns or roll yield, frequently prove inadequate to uncover the rich and latent
patterns embedded in data. For instance, Novy-Marx (2012) show that intermediate-horizon
past performance can beat the momentum based on recent performance in forecasting t + 1
returns, underscoring hidden temporal patterns that static formulaic metrics miss.
    To capture these richer dynamics, we introduce a novel image-based deep visualiza-
tion (DV) framework. First, we transform futures price trajectories and daily evolving
term-structure surfaces of commodity futures—varying continuously across maturities and
days—into different types of images, including images of return trajectories and term struc-
ture multi-day heatmaps. We then employ convolutional neural networks to extract latent
and return-predictive patterns. From these images, we derive four deep-visualized predic-
tors, including DV-based basis (bsDV ), basis momentum (bmDV ), momentum (momDV ), and
skewness (skDV ), each of which consistently outperforms its traditional analogue in commod-
ity futures return predictability. Constructing a DV-based factor model that relies solely on
our deep visualized predictors can better explain the cross-sectional variation of commodity
futures returns than the existing benchmark model.
    The need for this approach is evident in two dimensions of complexity. First, two se-
ries with identical cumulative returns can follow divergent paths—regime switches, signed
jumps, and shifts in volatility leave distinct visual “fingerprints” (Figure 1). A deeper man-
ifestation of this complexity lies in the structure of the futures curves. These curves are
utilized to construct the basis and basis momentum of commodity futures. Specifically, they
vary significantly along two dimensions—(i) the term structure (across contracts of differ-

                                              1
ent maturities) and (ii) time at a daily frequency—exhibiting rich “mosaic” patterns across
the resulting time-term structure surface (see Cong, Feng, He, and Wang (2024)). Conse-
quently, the basis and basis momentum, as formulaic features defined at a single point in
time, struggle to capture the full information richness embedded in this mosaic pattern. In
contrast, the complex mosaic pattern of the futures surface can be effectively visualized as
heatmaps, as illustrated in Figures 3 and 4. Given this inherent complexity, it is unlikely
that constructing commodity factors through simple fixed-formulaic combinations of returns
and basis can fully exploit the nuanced information contained therein.
   To capture mosaic patterns embedded in commodity futures data and harness the virtue
of complexity, our study represents in image for each of the futures term structure sur-
faces and the trajectories of past cumulative returns. We then employ image recognition
techniques derived from deep neural networks (DV hereafter) to extract latent, return-
predictive patterns from traditional commodity data. Our approach leads to a group of
four novel image-based commodity futures return predictors, including DV-based momen-
tum (momDV ), skewness (skDV ), basis momentum (bmDV ), and basis (bsDV ), respectively.
We find that each of the four DV-based factors achieves a high out-of-sample Sharpe ra-
tio and contributes independent and incremental information beyond their corresponding
formulaic analogs and any of well-documented commodity futures return predictors.
   Based on the return predictability of all four DV-based commodity return predictors, we
construct a novel image-based commodity four-factor model comprising deep-visualized mo-
mentum (M OMDV ), skewness (SKDV ), basis momentum (BMDV ), and carry (CARRYDV )
factors. All four DV-based factors are constructed as the spread portfolios of longing and
shorting commodity futures falling in extreme quartiles of momDV , skDV , bmDV , and bsDV ,
respectively. Our novel image-based four-factor model can successfully explain the cross-
sectional variation in commodity futures returns and that of testing assets formed with
respect to commodity return predictors such as in Bakshi, Gao, and Rossi (2019).
   We select momDV , skDV , bmDV , and bsDV as the focal predictors for deep-visualization


                                             2
not as a result of data mining or ex post performance considerations, but because the vi-
sual forms of these inputs—namely return trajectories and term structure surfaces—are the
most naturally suited to image-based analysis. Their representations are highly structured
and intuitive, offering clear manifestations of the underlying “commodity mosaic” patterns
that our approach seeks to exploit. As such, these factors serve as the most economically
meaningful and methodologically appropriate candidates for extracting latent, non-linear
predictive signals through deep visual learning.
   When constructing these DV-based factors, our approach extracts richer information
from the same set of commodity futures data while preserving the original economic intuition
underlying each conventional factor. Specifically, for the construction of the CARRYDV and
BMDV factors, we use the next-period formulaic basis and basis momentum as prediction
targets (labels), training convolutional neural networks (CNNs) to learn the most informative
patterns embedded in the futures surfaces and get the predicted values of basis and basis
momentum. The predictive values of basis (bsDV ) and basis momentum (bmDV ) are then
used to construct CARRYDV and BMDV factors as the spread portfolios longing and shorting
commodity futures fallen in the extreme bsDV or bmDV quartiles. Although we employ deep
neural networks, our methodology is effectively equivalent to forming expectations of basis
and basis momentum via conditional information, in our case, via image representation.
   Similarly, for the M OMDV and SKDV factors, we follow the methodology of Jiang, Kelly,
and Xiu (2023) and Murray, Xia, and Xiao (2024), encoding the 12-month trailing cumu-
lative returns for each commodity into a single image. We use next-period returns and
non-overlapping next-period return skewness as the labels to supervise CNNs to extract
return- and skewness-predictive features from these images, resulting in CNN-based momen-
tum and skewness expectations (momDV and skDV ). The M OMDV and SKDV factors are
consequently constructed as the spread portfolios of holding commodity futures with extreme
momDV and skDV . Before approaching the return predictability of our deep-visualized char-
acteristics (bs, bm, mom, sk), we first design tests to validate them as measures of their


                                             3
formulaic counterparts (bsDV , bmDV , momDV , skDV ). First, we find that each of our DV-
based factors exhibits strong correlations with the original formulaic factors. To be specific,
M OMDV and CARRYDV deliver non-negligible correlations of 0.5 and 0.52 with BGR’s
momentum and carry factors while the correlations between BMDV (SKDV ) and the basis
momentum (skewness) factor approach 0.51 (0.67).
   More importantly, we find that all the deep visualized characteristics, bsDV , bmDV ,
momDV , and skDV , serve as stronger predictors of their corresponding future character-
istics than their formulaic counterparts. For example, in pooled regression settings, bsDV
(bmDV ) measured at month t exhibits a significantly stronger positive association with the
future basis (basis momentum) compared to the contemporaneous basis itself. Similarly,
momDV not only predicts future cumulative returns but also subsumes the predictive power
of mom. That is, our deep visualization-based cumulative returns, skewness, basis, and basis
momentum are sufficient statistics of their corresponding expected future counterparts, even
compared with the formulaic characteristics.
   Having validated our deep visualized measures, we turn to investigating their return
predictability. Specifically, M OMDV (SKDV ) delivers a substantial annualized average raw
return of 13.2% (-15.5%) and a factor-adjusted return of 7.19% (-12.37%), all statistically
significant at the levels of 1% and 5%. Additionally, BMDV and CARRYDV yield annual-
ized factor-adjusted returns of 13.6% and 12.5%, respectively, while preserving significant
correlations of 0.51 and 0.67 with the original basis momentum and skewness factors as in
Boons and Prado (2019) and Fernandez-Perez, Frijns, Fuertes, and Miffre (2018).
   Given the significant return predictability of each individual four factors, we propose
a novel DV-based factor model for commodity futures markets comprising our DV-based
factors and the conventional market factor. Before approaching the specific factor model, a
natural question is whether each of the DV-based factors provides independent information
against their DV-based counterparts. We investigate this issue by performing spanning
regressions of one DV factor on the other three sequentially. We find that the profit of


                                               4
holding the CARRYDV factor is fully absorbed by the other three DV-based factors while
M OMDV , SKDV , and BMDV all provide independent information against each other.
   That is, CARRYDV is redundant relative to the other three DV-based factors, which
is analogous to the evidence that formulaic-based basis is driven out by basis momentum
(Boons and Prado (2019)). We then formalize our DV-based four-factor model comprising
of the market factor, M OMDV , SKDV , and BMDV . We find that the ex-post annualized
Sharpe ratio of the tangency portfolio spanned by our four-factor model is as high as 1.108,
whereas the annualized Sharpe ratio of the tangency portfolio spanned by the three factors
of BGR is only 0.624. When we combine BGR three factors with our DV-based factors,
the Sharpe ratio of the tangency portfolio from these six factors does not improve from our
four-factor model. Beyond that, the total portfolio weights of our four factors among the six
factors is as high as 0.91, implying a dominating role of our DV-based factor model.
   We then perform multiple formal asset pricing tests to justify the pricing power of our
four-factor model. To begin with, we construct a total of 28 commodity futures portfolios
with a strong factor structure as our test assets, including characteristic-sorted portfolios and
momentum and carry-managed portfolios of Bakshi, Gao, and Rossi (2019). We find that our
four-factor model can well explain the “true” pricing factors extracted from testing assets via
the principal component analysis (PCA) and the risk-premium principal component analysis
(RP-PCA, Lettau and Pelger, 2020b). In a second set of tests, we examine whether our four
factors are priced risk factors via directly estimating the SDF loadings of various factors
using a GMM approach (see Chapter 13, Cochrane, 2005a). The SDF loadings on the four
factors are always statistically significant no matter whether we only consider our four-factor
model or the six-factor model that includes BGR factors. The χ2 tests show that our four-
factor model performs very well in pricing the above 28 commodity futures portfolios and
the improvement is negligible by adding BGR factors to our four-factor model.
   Our final address is to systematically examine the rationale of our novel deep-visualized-
based factors. First, we investigate whether the factors derive their performance from the


                                               5
channel of market frictions. Our empirical evidence implies that none of our four deep
visualization-based factors loads on proxies of market frictions, including return volatility,
idiosyncratic volatility, and funding illiquidity (see Jia, Liu, Wu, and Yan (2024)), refusing
market frictions or limit-to-arbitrage as a potential channel. Our further evidence reveals
that the performance of CARRYDV and BMDV is derived from the risk of bearing macroe-
conomic uncertainties. To be specific, the performance of CARRYDV and BMDV is signif-
icantly more pronounced during recessions, loads negatively on global volatility and global
risk aversion (Bekaert, Engstrom, and Xu (2022)), providing a hedge against macroeconomic
risk. On the other hand, consistent with BGR and Fernandez-Perez, Frijns, Fuertes, and
Miffre (2018), we find that the performance of M OMDV and SKDV hinges on investors’
arbitrage activities.



2     Position in the Literature

This study makes important contributions to at least three strands of the existing literature.
In the first place, our research joins recent studies that extract return-predictive signals from
image representation of traditional finance data. For instance, Jiang, Kelly, and Xiu (2023)
and Murray, Xia, and Xiao (2024) convert stock price and volume data into candlestick-like
images and apply CNN to extract return-predictive patterns. Likewise, Kelly, Kuznetsov,
Malamud, and Xu (2023) leverage deep learning methods to analyze volatility surfaces in
the options market, revealing the return predictability by visualizing the volatility surface of
the option. In addition, this strand of literature goes beyond the stock return predictability.
Garfinkel, Hribar, and Hsiao (2024) extract predictive patterns for post-earnings announce-
ment drifts from visualized corporate earnings paths while Jia, Li, Zhang, and Zhao (2025)
time the zoo of stock anomalies (Cochrane (2011)) by visualizing the trajectories of each
equity factors as documented in Chen and Zimmermann (2022).
    Our research extends the above line of research along at least two key dimensions. First,



                                               6
to the best of our knowledge, we are among the first to estimate a factor pricing model that
relies exclusively on image-derived inputs. Specifically, our findings show that an image-
based asset pricing model—constructed solely from visual representations of commodity
data—can effectively explain the cross-sectional variation in commodity futures returns.
This methodology serves as a visual counterpart to the narrative asset pricing framework
proposed by Bybee, Kelly, and Su (2023), offering a novel and complementary perspective
on extracting pricing-relevant information. Second, the construction of our CARRYDV and
BMDV factors is also among the first to explicitly identify and leverage mosaic patterns, as
initiated in Cong, Feng, He, and Wang (2024), embedded within the dynamic term structure
of commodity futures. This approach highlights the significant potential of image recognition
techniques in capturing complex and otherwise latent structures in financial data.
   Our paper can also be positioned within the fast-moving literature that seeks to develop
(latent) factor models for pricing cross-sectional asset returns. Among these studies, Büchner
and Kelly (2022) and Kelly, Pruitt, and Su (2019) use firm characteristics as instruments to
model time-varying factor loadings via instrumental principal component analysis (PCA).
Kozak, Nagel, and Santosh (2020) show that the PCA of anomaly portfolios performs as
well as reduced-form factor models in explaining anomaly portfolios. Lettau and Pelger
(2020a) and Lettau and Pelger (2020b), among others, propose a regularized estimation of
risk premia—risk-premia PCA (RP-PCA)—which identifies factors with small time-series
variance that are nonetheless useful in the cross-section.
   Other studies impose non-linear structures on the stochastic discount factor (SDF).
Specifically, Chen, Pelger, and Zhu (2024) adopt a generative adversarial network (GAN)
framework to estimate the SDF, explaining cross-sectional stock returns through the con-
ditional moment restrictions implied by the no-arbitrage condition. Cong, Feng, He, and
He (2025) employ panel trees with a maximum Sharpe ratio-based economic splitting rule
to construct the SDF, while Feng, He, Polson, and Xu (2024) develop a latent asset pric-
ing model by minimizing aggregate realized pricing errors. Our research contributes to this


                                              7
strand of literature as a first attempt to develop a commodity futures pricing model using
the approach of image representation and recognition.
    Finally, this study also contributes to the literature on the return predictability of com-
modity futures. On one hand, we document that reimagining conventional commodity fu-
tures data and recognizing their image representations through neural networks designed for
pattern recognition can generate a set of novel deep-visualized factors. These factors pro-
vide independent information beyond their formulaic counterparts, including carry (Koijen,
Moskowitz, Pedersen, and Vrugt (2018)), momentum (BGR), skewness (Fernandez-Perez,
Frijns, Fuertes, and Miffre (2018)), and basis momentum (Boons and Prado (2019)). Fur-
thermore, our deep-visualized four-factor asset pricing model outperforms conventional com-
modity futures pricing models, such as that of Bakshi, Gao, and Rossi (2019), in explaining
the cross-sectional variation of commodity futures returns. In other words, our model can
potentially serve as a novel benchmark for future research in commodity futures pricing.
    The rest of this article is organized as follows: Section 2 illustrates our process of building
deep visualization-based factors. Section 3 discusses the return predictability of each of
the deep visualization-based factors. Section 4 presents rigorous asset pricing tests for our
deep visualization-based four-factor model and provides initial evidence on the mechanism.
Section 5 concludes. Additional implementation details are provided in Section 3.3.



3     Constructing Deep Visualization-Based Factors

In this section, we describe the complete process of constructing our factors based on deep
visualization. First, we introduce the data source and the definition of commodity charac-
teristics that are used to construct commodity common factors in Section 3.1. Section 3.2
discusses the motivation and detailed steps in representing the conventional commodity data
into two specific types of images. Finally, in Section 3.3, we define the specific architectures
for each specific neural network.

3.1    Data, Commodity Futures Returns, and the Basis

We collect data on exchange-traded, liquid commodity futures contracts from Thomson
Reuters Datastream, spanning from January 1973 to January 2024. Our final sample in-
cludes 25 commodities with active futures markets in four major categories-namely agricul-
ture, energy, livestock, and metal: Cocoa, Coffee, Copper, Corn, Cotton, Crude Oil, Feeder
Cattle, Gold, Heating Oil, Lean Hogs, Live Cattle, Natural Gas, Oats, Orange Juice, Pal-
ladium, Platinum, Propane, RBOB Gasoline, Rough Rice, Silver, Soybean Meal, Soybean
Oil, Soybeans, Sugar, and Wheat. For each commodity and available contract maturity, the
dataset provides daily observations of open, low, high, and closed prices, as well as open
interest and trading volume. Our selection of 25 commodity futures aligns with that used in
BGR, excluding four contracts: Barley, Lumber, Unleaded Gasoline, and Pork Belly. Barley
and unleaded gasoline are omitted because of their limited sample periods, 1994 to 2003
and 1985 to 2006, respectively. Lumber and Pork Belly are not included due to the data
limitation. The number of commodities available in our sample ranges from a minimum of
18 in 1973 to 25 since January 2008.

the present value of the futures price to eliminate the counterparty risk. This cash amount,
which is used as collateral, earns the risk-free rate. For commodity i, we denote the futures
price at time t with maturity T as Fi;t;T . The futures excess return of longing this contract
for one period is defined as

Additionally, following Fernandez-Perez, Frijns, Fuertes, and Miffre (2018), the return skew-

   The Necessity of Image Representation: A closer examination of the dynamics
underlying each of the conventional month-end measures—mom, bs, bm, and sk—reveals
significantly richer patterns along two dimensions. First, the trajectories of past return pat-
terns vary markedly across commodities and over time, yet such information is not captured
by mom or sk. At any given time t, two commodities with identical cumulative 12-month
returns (mom) may follow distinctly different return paths within the measurement win-
dow. A commodity may realize most of its gains early in the window, rendering momentum
merely an “echo” of distant past performance (Goyal and Wahal, 2015). Conversely, returns
concentrated toward the end of the window are more consistent with the intuitive notion of
momentum. Similarly, commodities with different return dynamics can still exhibit identical


                                              10
return skewness, highlighting the limitations of sk in reflecting the underlying variation.
   Second, the term structures of commodity futures prices fluctuate throughout the trading
days within each month, extending well beyond what a single month-end snapshot can
capture. Aggregating these daily term structures yields a richer, surface-like representation
of their dynamics. Relying solely on month-end measures such as bs and bm sacrifices
this granularity for the sake of parsimony, potentially overlooking important intra-month
variations. The complex patterns along both dimensions underscore the relevance of the
notion that “a picture is worth a thousand words” in financial data analysis, as emphasized
in Obaid and Pukthuanthong (2022). To better convey this richness, we transform the data
into visual representations. Section 3.2 outlines the procedure used to encode the commodity
futures returns and the term structure as separate images.


3.2    Image Representation of Commodity Futures Data

We use two categories of images to represent commodity futures data. First, to visualize
the trajectories of cumulative commodity futures returns, we follow Murray, Xia, and Xiao
(2024) (hereafter MXX) and convert the return trajectories into black-and-white candlestick
charts. These candlestick charts are then fed into deep neural networks for pattern recogni-
tion. Second, to represent the dynamics of the daily frequency term structure of commodity
futures, we constructed a two-dimensional surface spanned by contract maturity and trading
day in chronological order. Each point on this surface corresponds to the futures price (or
return) for a given maturity on a specific trading day. The resulting pattern, with values
varying across both dimensions, resembles a mosaic-like pattern and is encoded as a heatmap.
The heatmaps are utilized as input for our image recognition models. Detailed discussions
of the two types of images are presented as follows.

   The Image Representation of Return Trajectories: To map the MXX approach
to the visualization of cumulative returns, we construct, for each commodity, its cumulative
return throughout the entire time series at the monthly frequency. We then create candlestick

                                             11
graphs by truncating the entire time series in the following two steps. First, we standardize
the past cumulative returns within each chart by setting the price to $1 on the first month
appearing in the plotted range. This normalization translates cumulative past returns into a
relative scale for comparison purposes. To be specific, the objects fed into a neural network
are truncated plots of the cumulative futures returns for commodities j over months t − N
through t − 1, which we denote as CRj,1 , ..., CRj,12 . That is, CRj,k is the cumulative futures
return over the period of k months, covering months t − N through t − N + k − 1. In
our images, each CRj,k bar spans three pixels in width. The benchmark images fed into
neural networks consist of concatenated monthly cumulative bars over a consecutive 12-
month interval, corresponding to one-year cumulative return trajectories. Consequently, an
image representing N months has a width of 3 × N pixels.
   After concatenation, all images of the same time span are standardized to a fixed scale,
height, and width. For instance, all cumulative return plots share a uniform dimension of
36 × 36. Compared to a single number summarizing cumulative returns, our image rep-
resentation encodes the full shape of the return accumulation over the truncated window.
This format captures not only the level and slope of factor dynamics but also the curvature
of their trajectories. As illustrated in the four panels of Figure 1, CRj,1 denotes the com-
modity futures return in the first month shown, and more generally, CRj,k represents the
cumulative return of commodity j over the first k months on the plot. Each panel presents
a real cumulative return trajectory from our commodity futures sample, all converging to
a final cumulative return of 2%. Panel A shows a relatively smooth and monotonic accu-
mulation of returns. In contrast, Panel B exhibits a downward-sloping path, while Panels
C and D highlight the presence of local jumps and crashes in the return accumulation pro-
cess. Although all trajectories culminate in the same cumulative return, their intermediate
paths differ substantially, suggesting that the shape of the trajectory may contain valuable
information beyond the endpoint alone.

   The Image Representation of Skewness Trajectories: In addition to visualizing

                                              12
the return trajectories of commodity futures, we adopt a similar approach to represent the
trajectories of return skewness. As discussed in Section 3.1, we have already constructed a
time series of return skewness for each commodity future. These time series are segmented
into 12-month rolling windows for each commodity j, spanning months t − N through t − 1,
which we denote as skewj,1 , ..., skewj,12 . In other words, each image captures the return
skewness over 12 consecutive months. Each skewj,k represents the skewness of the daily
returns calculated at the end of month k, using the return data from months t − N to
t − N + k − 1. To generate the images, each skewj,k bar is assigned a fixed width of three
pixels following the same setting as the images of cumulative returns.
   The resulting benchmark images used as input for the neural networks are composed of
concatenated monthly skewness bars over a 12-month window, reflecting one-year skewness
trajectories. Consequently, an image representing N months has a width of 3 × N pixels.
In our benchmark case, as shown in Figure 2, all cumulative return plots share a uniform
dimension of 36 × 36. Figure 2 reveals distinctive patterns through four parallel plots. In
particular, although converging to a value of 0.3, the return skewness trajectories exhibit
substantial variation across the plots, as indicated by the final bars in each of the plotted
sequences. For instance, the Image A in top left reveals a commodity with a mild increasing
return skewness to 0.3 while the one in Panel D indicates the return skewness of commodity
D decreases sharply from around 3 to 0.3 from month t − 12 to month t − 1. These observa-
tions suggest that the shape of the trajectory may contain valuable information beyond the
formulaic-based return skewness at the endpoint alone.

   The Mosaic-Like Images for Futures Curves: To map the temporal and cross-
sectional dynamics of the commodity futures term structure into an image format, we design a
two-dimensional matrix-based representation, which we refer to as the “mosaic-like” images.
Each image is constructed by stacking daily snapshots of the futures curve over a rolling
window of trading days. Along the horizontal axis, we index time by trading days, and
along the vertical axis, we index contract maturities (e.g., front-month, 2nd-month, ..., 12th-

                                              13
month). For each trading day, the vertical slice of the image corresponds to the futures
price (or log return) across different maturities, forming a cross-sectional view of the term
structure. By horizontally concatenating these slices over time, we generate a heatmap-like
image where each pixel represents the price (or return) of a particular maturity on a specific
trading day, as illustrated in Figures 3 for basis and 4 for basis momentum.
   The resulting image captures the joint evolution of the term structure along two dimen-
sions: time-series variation and cross-maturity dynamics. The color intensity (or grayscale
value) of each pixel encodes the magnitude of the corresponding price or return, thereby re-
flecting not only level shifts but also structural changes such as slope steepening/flattening
and curvature inversions. To mitigate scale effects, we normalize pixel values using min-max
scaling or z-score standardization within a rolling window.
   To improve comparability with carry in Koijen, Moskowitz, Pedersen, and Vrugt (2018),
our graphical representation utilizes the same set of underlying variables as those used in
Equation (3). The key difference lies in the format: While Equation (3) provides a static,
point-in-time measure, Figure 3 visualizes the historical evolution of these variables across
both the time and maturity dimensions using a heatmap. This dynamic visualization reveals
richer structural patterns embedded in the term structure over time. Likewise, to maintain
consistency with the construction of the bm measure in Boons and Prado (2019), we restrict
our graphical analysis of the basis to the first-, second-, and third-nearby futures contracts
and create the heatmap-like image as shown as Figure 4. To be specific, when setting the
images for bm, each “mosaic” element as shown in Figure 4 represents the difference between

   These mosaic-like images are particularly well-suited for the recognition of convolutional
neural networks (CNNs), which are adept at extracting spatial patterns such as contiguity,
trend persistence, and localized shocks. By encoding the term structure into this grid-like
format, the model can learn to detect stylized patterns of backwardation, contango, and

Compared to traditional factor modeling approaches that reduce the term structure to a few
summary statistics (e.g., slope, curvature), our approach retains the full cross-maturity panel

3.3    Image Recognition via Convolutional Neural Networks

Once the image representations of return trajectories and the term structure are constructed,
we use non-overlapping next-period commodity characteristics—momentum (mom), basis
slope (bs), basis momentum (bm), and skewness (sk)—as target variables (labels) to guide
the supervised learning process. The model outputs are the expected values of these char-
acteristics, denoted as momDV , bsDV , bmDV , and skDV , where the subscript DV stands for
“deep visualization”. This framework goes beyond a purely statistical exercise and aligns
with the economic intuition that investors form expectations about future returns and curve
dynamics based on evolving patterns in commodity futures data.
   Unlike previous studies that employ traditional machine learning methods or shallow
neural networks (e.g., Bali, Beckmeyer, Moerke, and Weigert (2023)), our approach leverages
deep convolutional neural networks (CNN) to fully exploit the rich structure of image-based
inputs described in Section 3.2. As argued by Kelly, Malamud, and Zhou (2024), more
complex models are better suited to capture intricate patterns, making CNNs a natural fit
for our setting. CNNs are particularly effective due to their ability to extract localized and
hierarchical features from structured image data. As noted by LeCun, Bengio, and Hinton
(2015), convolutional filters excel at identifying spatial patterns through parameter sharing
and translational invariance. In our context, this allows the model to detect economically
meaningful signals—such as local jumps, crashes, or persistent term structure shifts—that
may be predictive of future factor dynamics (Zhang, Lipton, Li, and Smola (2023)).
   Guided by the well-known No Free Lunch Theorem in the territory of machine learning
(Bishop and Bishop (2023)), which posits that no single model performs best across all possi-

                                              15
ble problems, we employ three distinct neural network architectures tailored to the prediction
of the four different commodity characteristics. Given the heterogeneity in the statistical
properties and economic interpretations of momentum, basis slope, basis momentum, and
skewness, a one-size-fits-all approach is unlikely to be optimal. Instead of designing a com-
plex and catch-all network for all categories of images, we design different neural network
structures to recognize mosaic-like images for basis and basis momentum and trajectory-like
images for momentum and skewness. Our approach aligns the architecture with the nature of
the target variable, allowing for better pattern recognition and generalization performance
across tasks. The network structures for image recognition of mom, sk, bm, and bs are
presented as follows.

   Network structures for image recognition of mom and sk: To recognize images of
trajectory-like images for mom and sk, we follow Murray, Xia, and Xiao (2024) and average
the model forecasts of four neural network architectures to generate DV-based return and
skewness forecasts. The four architectures include: a feed-forward neural network (FNN),
a convolutional neural network (CNN), a long short-term memory network (LSTM), and a
convolutional neural network with long short-term memory (CNNLSTM). Specifically, the
CNN architecture in our study consists of a convolutional layer with 64 filters and a kernel
size of 5, followed by a max pooling layer and a fully connected (FC) layer with 64 units. SiLU
(sigmoid-weighted linear unit) activations are used throughout to enhance representational
flexibility while preserving training stability.
   While CNNs are well-suited for recognizing static images with limited sequential depen-
dencies, their capacity to model temporal dynamics is inherently constrained. To address this
limitation, we augment the baseline CNN architecture with a recurrent module, specifically
incorporating a Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) layer. The resulting architecture com-
prises a convolutional layer (64 filters, kernel size = 5), followed by max pooling, an LSTM
layer with 64 hidden units, and a fully connected layer with 64 units. This architecture is
designed to capture evolving sequential dependencies in trajectories, which is typical in the

                                                   16
images of cumulative returns or skewness, while preserving the spatial feature extraction
capabilities of the convolutional layers.
   The feedforward neural network (NN) follows a standard multilayer perceptron architec-
ture, comprising three fully connected layers with 64 units each, followed by a final output
layer. ReLU activations are applied between layers, with a dropout rate of 0.1 incorporated
for regularization. This model serves as a benchmark architecture for capturing nonlinear
relationships within vectorized return skewness features, without explicitly accounting for
spatial or temporal dependencies. In contrast, we also implement a long short-term memory
(LSTM) network as a sequential alternative designed to directly model raw skewness and
cumulative return time series. The LSTM architecture consists of a single recurrent layer
with 64 units, followed by a fully connected output layer. Input features are reshaped into
temporal sequences, enabling the model to capture long-range dependencies in time-varying
distributional characteristics. To summarize, the cumulative return trajectories shown in
Figure 1 and the skewness trajectories in Figure 2 are used as inputs to each of the four
neural network architectures. Each model produces DV-based expectations for momentum
(mom) and skewness (sk) measures. The final DV-based characteristics, denoted as momDV
and skDV , are constructed as the simple averages across the outputs of the four models.

   Network Architectures for Mosaic-Like Images of bm and bs: We design two
specialized convolutional neural network (CNN) architectures to extract predictive features
from mosaic-style visualizations of the basis (bs) and basis momentum (bm) factors. For the
bs predictor, we construct a multi-branch CNN tailored to extract temporal features from
mosaic-like representations of basis signals. The architecture begins with parallel 1 × 3 and
1 × 5 convolutional layers (each with 64 filters), enabling the extraction of local patterns
at multiple temporal resolutions. These branches are followed by a squeeze-and-excitation
(SE) block and a depthwise separable convolution layer, which together enhance channel-wise
feature recalibration while maintaining computational efficiency.
   The resulting feature maps are merged and passed through a fully connected (FC) layer

                                             17
with SiLU activations. Residual connections are incorporated to facilitate gradient propaga-
tion and support hierarchical temporal feature integration. Mosaic-like visualizations of bs
exhibit localized temporal dynamics distributed over a spatial grid. The use of multi-branch
convolutions allows the network to extract features across varying time horizons, while the
SE block adaptively reweights channels to emphasize salient temporal patterns. Residual
connections further stabilize training and preserve multi-scale representations, enhancing
the model’s ability to learn from non-standard visual encodings. The full architecture is
illustrated in Figure 5.
   For the bm predictor, we propose a lightweight CNN tailored to the spatial structure
of basis momentum heatmaps. The architecture begins with a 2 × 3 convolutional layer
(16 filters), followed by a 1 × 3 convolution (32 filters), a pooling layer, and a final FC
layer. ReLU activations are applied after each convolution and the FC layer to introduce
non-linearity. This compact design is optimized for extracting short-range, cross-channel
interactions relevant to basis momentum dynamics, while ensuring computational efficiency.
The network architecture for bm is shown in Figure 6.
   The architectural distinction between the models used for bm and bs is guided by the
differing visual structures of the input images: bs mosaics are narrow and temporally aligned,
favoring multi-branch designs that resolve horizon-dependent variation; in contrast, bm mo-
saics are spatially broader, requiring horizontally-aware filters and early pooling to capture
cross-horizon momentum patterns efficiently. This tailored alignment between visual struc-
ture and network architecture ensures that each model captures the most salient predictive
signals embedded in the spatial organization of the underlying financial data.

   Model Training: The above neural network processes aim to minimize a loss function
L that aggregates forecast errors through an iterative gradient-based optimizer. We train
all models with the Adam optimizer using the default parameters. The two loss functions
that we consider are the mean squared error (MSE, L = Σωj ϵ2j ) and the mean absolute error
(MAE, L = Σωj |ϵj |), where ωj is the weight assigned to observation j in the training data,

                                             18
ϵj is the error, which is the difference between the predicted value and the realized value of
the dependent variable. The summation is taken over all observations in the training data.
    Since our training sample dates back to the very early age of asset pricing research,
the amount of commodities varies substantially over months. Our CNN architectures could
potentially be affected by the month with more valid commodity observations under the
universal equal-weighting scheme (EW). To address this concern, we follow Murray, Xia, and
Xiao (2024) and consider one alternative weighting scheme, EWPM, by equally weighting
each month and within each month, equally weighting each valid commodity. To sum up,
when generating DV-based momentum and skewness (momDV and skDV ) expectations , the
outputs from each of the four architectures (CNN, CNNLSTM, LSTM, NN) are the simple
average of the forecasts from four variations, including two loss functions (MSE, MAE)
and two weighting schemes (EW and EWPM). The outputs are then averaged for the final
outputs. When generating DV-based basis and basis momentum expectation (bsDV and
bmDV ), the outputs are the simple averages from the four variations from (MSE, MAE) and
two weighting schemes (EW and EWPM).

4.2    The Return Predictability of Deep-Visualized Characteristics

To test whether the deep-visualized commodity characteristic predicts future cross-sectional
commodity futures returns, we utilize two standard empirical methods: portfolio sorts and
cross-sectional regressions of Fama and MacBeth (1973). In portfolio sorts, we rank the com-
modities at the end of each month by each of the four deep-visualized characteristics and
group them into quartile portfolios. We compute the equal-weighted next-month portfolio
returns. An increasing (decreasing) pattern in portfolio returns from the lowest to the highest
quartile would indicate positive (negative) return predictability of the commodity charac-
teristics. If portfolio sorts indicate commodity futures return predictability, we next check
whether the evidence can be explained by other competing commodity return predictors.
   To do so, we perform sequential double portfolio sorts, where we first sort the commodity
futures into halves based on a specific control variable such as past cumulative commodity
returns and then within each half we further sort the commodity futures into halves based
on each of the DV-based commodity characteristics. If the control variable can explain the
return predictability of the characteristics, we expect that the monotonic pattern in the
portfolio returns in these characteristics to be attenuated within each half of the control
variable. Previous studies also indicate portfolio sorts become ineffective when examining
multiple control variables simultaneously (Freyberger, Neuhierl, and Weber, 2020). To ad-
dress this concern, we employ the cross-sectional Fama and MacBeth (1973) regressions,
where the future return is the dependent variable and the independent variables include DV-
based characteristics and the control variables. In the case that DV-based characteristics
provide additional return predictive power in the presence of control variables, we expect
the average coefficient on DV-based characteristics to be statistically significant. For both
portfolio sorts and cross-sectional regressions, we use the Newey and West (1987) adjusted


                                              22
standard errors with four lags to compute the t-statistics.

presents the results for momDV . The average annualized return increases from -2.9% in
the lowest quartile to 10.3% in the highest. The high-minus-low (HML) spread portfolio

coefficient of 0.390. These results underscore that even though CM OM outperforms BGR,
momDV captures a return pattern closely aligned with CM OM .
   Panels B, C, and D present empirical evidence for skDV , bmDV , bsDV , respectively. The
results for skDV and bmDV are even more striking. The spread portfolios that are long

4.3     Asset Pricing Tests

Motivated by the strong return predictability of DV-based characteristics—specifically, momDV ,
skDV , and bmDV —we propose a new four-factor commodity pricing model. These DV-based
characteristics exhibit robust predictive power that cannot be explained by conventional
commodity return predictors or their DV-based counterparts. The model includes the tra-
ditional market factor along with the three DV-based factors, aiming to better capture the
cross-sectional variation in commodity futures returns.
   In Subsection 4.3.1, we assess the incremental contribution of the DV-based factors by
comparing the efficient frontiers spanned with and without their inclusion. We further in-
vestigate the latent factor structure of commodity futures to evaluate whether the DV-based
characteristics represent underlying systematic risk factors. Section 4.3.2 presents formal
asset pricing tests, including Fama–MacBeth regressions and GMM estimation, to evaluate
the pricing performance and statistical validity of the proposed four-factor model.


4.3.1   Efficient Frontier and Factor Structure

We exactly follow BGR to build CAV G , CCARRY , and CM OM for our sample as the base
factors. We then construct asset pricing factors, for each DV-based characteristic, including
momDV , skDV , and bmDV , as the spread portfolio of longing and shorting the commod-
ity futures falling in the extreme quartiles. Specifically, for DV-based characteristics with
significant positive return predictability, including momDV , bsDV , and bmDV , we long and
short the commodities in the highest and lowest quartiles to form spread portfolios as factors
named as M OMDV and BMDV , respectively. For skDV , the DV-based characteristic with
significant negative return predictability, we long and short the commodities in the lowest
and highest quartiles to form the spread portfolios as the factor for skDV , named SKDV . In

and West (1987) adjustment or the Shanken (1992) standard errors (e.g., Cochrane (2005b)).

premium vector λ is related to b by λ = Σb, where Σ denotes the variance-covariance matrix of
factors Ft (see Chapter 13, Cochrane, 2005b). Because the number of test assets is relatively
large, the statistically optimal weighting matrix of the GMM estimation recommended by

the weighting matrix. Cochrane (2005b) argues that the using the identity matrix can lead
to more robust GMM estimates.2

4.4       Inspecting the Mechanism

Having established the return predictability of the three DV-based factors, BMDV , M OMDV ,
and SKDV , we proceed to investigate the underlying economic mechanisms from two com-
plementary perspectives. First, we assess whether our DV-based factors and their formulaic
counterparts in prior literature embed similar informational content. Second, we build on
prior literature by examining how the explanatory power of DV-based factors varies across
economically meaningful market states.
       In the first analysis, we find that, despite being constructed in a distinct methodology,
DV-based factors exhibit information content closely aligned with their formulaic counter-
parts. This is consistent with the high empirical correlations observed between DV-based
characteristics and their traditional analogues.3 Specifically, we document that M OMDV
is strongly associated with fluctuations in speculative activity, echoing the findings of Bak-
   3
    To conserve space and for the sake of brevity, empirical results that corroborate findings established in
prior literature are not reported. These results are available from the authors upon request.


                                                     32
shi, Gao, and Rossi (2019) on commodity momentum. Similarly, BMDV shows a robust
relationship with commodity market volatility and illiquidity, consistent with the mecha-
nisms proposed by Boons and Prado (2019) for basis momentum. In addition, SKDV is
linked to investors’ skewness preference and hedging pressure, in line with the evidence on
sk documented by Fernandez-Perez, Frijns, Fuertes, and Miffre (2018).
   Nonetheless, our empirical findings also suggest that none of our DV-based factors can
be fully accounted for by the mechanisms established in the existing literature. As for our
second set of investigation, we investigate whether the DV-based factors capture compensa-
tion for macroeconomic uncertainties. This line of inquiry is motivated by previous studies
showing that futures prices and open interest embed macroeconomic information and reflect
global trade dynamics (Hong and Yogo (2012); Ready, Roussanov, and Ward (2017)). On
one hand, we assess the sensitivity of factor returns to various categories of economic uncer-
tainty, as defined in Baker, Bloom, and Davis (2016). On the other hand, given the role of
international tensions in shaping global trade, we examine the dependence of factor returns
on the geopolitical risk represented by the proxy of U.S. and China tensions.
   To investigate the potential channels mentioned above, we estimate, for each commodity,
its exposure to economic uncertainties and other proxies in two steps following Jia, Liu, Wu,
and Yan (2024) among others. First, we take the first-order difference of policy uncertainty,
US-China tension, oil price, and public-sector investment. Then, we run 48-month rolling-
window univariate regressions of commodity futures returns on each first-order differences
to get the exposure of the commodity on certain uncertainties. Exposures to policy uncer-
tainty, tension, oil price, and public sector investment are denoted as β∆P U , β∆U S−China ,
βOil , and βP ubInv . We then perform sequential portfolio sorts first on one of the exposures
into halves and then, within each half, sort the commodities into halves based the DV-
based characteristics (momDV , skDV , and bmDV ). A high exposure to uncertainty measures
implies a statistically significant difference in the return predictability of these DV-based
characteristics between the high and low exposure regimes.


                                             33
   Table 10 reports the corresponding results for the economic policy uncertainty indices
and US-China tensions. Several patterns emerge. DV-based factors are significantly loaded
on equity-related uncertainty measures. Specifically, M OMDV earns higher returns during
periods of low uncertainty related to financial crises and oil market volatility, consistent with
the procyclical behavior documented in Daniel and Moskowitz (2016). In contrast, SKDV
shows stronger performance during periods of increased uncertainty, suggesting that it may
offer downside protection during turbulent market conditions. The behavior of BMDV varies
across macro states, performing better when volatility is low. More interestingly, Panel B
indicates that two of our three DV-based characteristics, including skDV and bmDV , perform
more pronounced among commodity futures with less exposure to the severity of US-China
tensions, indicating that DV-based factors co-move with uncertainty stemming from both
financial markets and geopolitical dynamics. Moreover, Panel C indicates that the return
predictability of momDV and skDV generate higher returns in periods of elevated oil price
volatility, highlighting the role of oil as a potential common macro driver of commodity
premia. Finally, as evident in Panel D, the return predictability of DV-based characteristics
tend to be more pronounced among the commodity futures with high exposure to public
sector investment, emphasizing the impact of macroeconomic fundamentals.
   Taken together, these results highlight the importance of state dependence in understand-
ing DV-based factor premia. Rather than being static anomalies, these premia reflect condi-
tional compensation when facing market frictions that vary with the broader economic and
policy environment. In tranquil markets, the return differentials narrow, consistent with re-
duced constraints and a lower demand for hedging. In contrast, in high-uncertainty regimes,
DV-based strategies exhibit elevated returns, either due to delayed arbitrage or increased
risk compensation. This dynamic interpretation not only enhances our understanding of DV
factors as systematic strategies but also underscores the necessity of incorporating regime
indicators when designing or timing such portfolios.

5     Concluding Remarks

This study introduces a novel methodology for constructing an image-based commodity
factor model by applying image recognition techniques derived from deep visualization (DV)
to conventional commodity futures data. Traditional formulaic measures, such as mom, sk,
bm, and bs, often fail to capture the rich and complex dynamics of commodity markets. In
contrast, we transform conventional commodity data into mosaic-style images, enabling the
extraction of latent structure and complexity.
    Leveraging neural networks designed for image recognition tasks, we derive four deep-
visualized characteristics: momDV , skDV , bmDV , and bsDV . These DV-based character-
istics demonstrate strong return predictability for the cross-section of commodity futures
and consistently outperform their traditional formula-based counterparts. Importantly, the
DV-based characteristics preserve the economic intuition that underpins their conventional
analogs. First, each DV-based characteristic exhibits a strong positive correlation with its
formulaic counterpart. Second, they are capable of forecasting the future evolution of tra-
ditional measures, even after accounting for contemporaneous values. Building on this, we
construct four DV-based factors, M OMDV , SKDV , BMDV , and CARRYDV , each formed
as a long–short portfolio based on extreme quartiles sorted by the corresponding DV-based
characteristics.
    We perform a series of rigorous asset pricing tests to evaluate the performance of our
image-based four-factor model. Our DV-based model, comprising M OMDV , SKDV , BMDV ,
and the market factor AV G, achieves a Sharpe ratio of more than 1.10, markedly higher
than that of the Bakshi, Gao, and Rossi (2019) three-factor (BGR) model, which posts a
Sharpe ratio of around 0.65. Augmenting the BGR model with our DV-based factor model
yields a six-factor model with a Sharpe ratio of 1.11, with around 90% of the portfolio weight
concentrated in our DV-based factors. That is, BGR can hardly add independent information
to the efficient frontier generated by our DV-based four-factor model. Furthermore, our
model effectively explains the cross section of returns on portfolios formed by established

                                             35
commodity characteristics, individual commodity contracts, and latent factors extracted via
PCA and RPPCA. These results highlight the robustness and explanatory power of our
deep-visualized factor structure and underscore the potential of combining deep learning
techniques with traditional asset pricing models.
   Additional analyses suggest that the performance of these DV-based factors is linked to
macroeconomic uncertainties. Beyond that, the predictive strength of three out of four DV-
based factors weakens significantly during periods of escalating US-China tensions, pointing
to a previously underexplored asset pricing channel: geopolitical relations. Taken together,
our findings demonstrate that deep visualization provides a powerful framework for uncov-
ering hidden structures in commodity markets, enhancing return predictability, and offering
new perspectives on the macroeconomic and geopolitical forces driving asset prices. This
approach opens new avenues for future research in machine learning–based asset pricing.
