Abstract
This study establishes an ordinary differential equation (ODE) model for contin-
uous-time portfolio optimisation which addresses time-dependent risk aversion in
mean-variance problems. The model includes practical constraints, including sector
limits, a quadratic transaction cost penalty, and a total transaction cost budget. The
Euler and fourth-order Runge-Kutta (RK4) methods were evaluated through nu-
merical experiments to determine their accuracy, stability, and convergence proper-
ties. This study demonstrates how different numerical approaches impact portfolio
performance, particularly when transaction costs are involved. The model under-
goes a sensitivity analysis to evaluate its robustness by testing risk aversion levels
and trading expenses. The model produces traditional mean-variance results when
costs are absent, but generates stable adaptive portfolio adjustments when costs are
included. The ODE-based method provides an efficient method for dynamically
managing portfolios under actual trading restrictions.

2 Introduction

Portfolio optimisation is an important activity in financial management, and its pur-
pose is to maximise returns and minimise risk. Many strategies and tools have been
proposed to solve this problem, each possessing certain advantages and disadvan-
tages. Particle Swarm Optimisation (PSO) has been successfully applied to portfolio
optimisation and has been found to be more efficient than GA, DP, and DEA in terms
of the Sharpe ratio and expected return (Das et al., 2023). For example, the MST
method is also found to be efficient in portfolio selection and allocation, especially in
the COVID-19 market crisis (Berouaga et al., 2023). For instance, alternative assets

such as Bitcoin can act as diversifiers in a portfolio during normal market conditions
and may also have hedge-to-safe haven properties. However, such assets should be
used with caution by risk-averse investors, as a high concentration in the portfolio
may lead to a high probability of losses in the market conditions (Bakry et al., 2021).
   The hidden Markov model has also been applied to distinguish between market
regimes and to design portfolios when there is uncertainty in the market, which could
assist in avoiding risk in left-tail events (Bae et al., 2014). In summary, several opti-
misation techniques and approaches have been proven effective in portfolio man-
agement. For example, the Black-Litterman model has been found to have better
out-of-sample performance than naive diversification and other strategies (Bessler
et al., 2017). Furthermore, entropy-based approaches have been found to be useful
in improving the diversification and robustness of portfolios (Pola, 2016). During
development, investors and portfolio managers must weigh risk tolerance, invest-
ment opportunities, and market trends when making decisions about optimisation
strategies.
   The mean-variance model is the most popular framework for describing the port-
folio choice problem because it allows one to quantify the diversification advantage
(Kim et al., 2021). Although it is known to have certain restrictions, it is still used
as a first approximation for making asset-allocation decisions in the real world. The
importance of the model lies in its practical use and further development. Asset man-
agers apply mean-variance optimisation, simulation, and a number of robust methods
for asset allocation purposes (Kim et al., 2021).
   Recent improvements have been made to reduce some of the shortcomings of the
model, such as its sensitivity to estimation errors. For example, machine learning
theory has been used to produce more robust allocations (Bruder et al., 2013), and
the Resampled Efficiency TM techniques have introduced Monte Carlo methods to
better represent investment information uncertainty (Michaud & Michaud, 2007). In
conclusion, although the mean-variance model is not without limitations, it is nev-
ertheless an extremely important model for portfolio optimisation. It is still being
developed, for example, by incorporating unsupervised machine learning methods
such as clustering analysis (Tayalı, 2020) and other models, including the Black-Lit-
terman model (Bessler et al., 2017), to address some of the deficiencies of the initial
model. These developments show that the mean-variance model is still relevant and
can be easily adopted in current portfolio theories and practices.

Research Question The main goal of this study is to assess if discretised under both
Euler and Runge-Kutta schemes, the newly proposed ODE-based dynamic mean-
variance approach provides significant benefits over conventional rebalancing
approaches. This study specifically investigates whether simplex constraints, sector
constraints, including transaction-cost penalties in the objective function, and finally
imposing both a penalty and a capped total cost produce improved performance in
risk management and net returns. First, this study examines the impact of various
discretisation schemes (Euler vs. RK4) on the numerical stability, convergence, and
real-time responsiveness of the gradient-flow solution for dynamic mean–variance
optimisation. In the context of time-varying market estimates, it is hypothesised that
higher-order methods (RK4) may result in reduced unintended portfolio turnover

compared with standard Euler steps. However, the empirical verification of this
effect remains an open research question. Second, this study investigates whether
incorporating sector-specific constraints can effectively mitigate overconcentration
in particular industries or asset classes without compromising the primary benefits
of a dynamic approach. This study examines whether, when the gradient-flow algo-
rithm is constrained to operate within both the standard simplex (i.e. fully invested,
non-negative weights summing to one) and an additional sector exposure cap, robust
performance is still observed or if the solution deteriorates, potentially overreact-
ing to short-term signals. Subsequently, this study examines the impact of explicitly
incorporating transaction cost penalties in the objective function on the temporal evo-
lution of the ODE solution and the frequency of trades. While it is well-established
that increased turnover can diminish realised returns when trading frictions are con-
sidered, it remains uncertain whether an adaptive ODE-based approach is sufficiently
’smooth’ to surpass static benchmarks net of costs. Furthermore, this study explores
whether imposing a cap on the total cumulative transaction cost (i.e. introducing a
total cost budget as a constraint) promotes even lower turnover or inadvertently con-
strains the system excessively, resulting in missed opportunities. Finally, this study
aims to investigate the overall robustness of these incremental dynamic allocations
when subjected to model- and parameter-uncertainties. This study seeks to ascer-
tain whether the smaller step-by-step adjustments inherent in an ODE-based solution
can mitigate the adverse effects of estimation errors more effectively than static or
simpler dynamic rebalancing. Ultimately, by examining the four variants (simplex
projection, Proximal projection, sector capping, cost penalty, and a full cost budget)
across both Euler and RK4 discrete methods, this investigation aims to draw gen-
eralisable conclusions about how ODE-driven mean–variance optimisation can be
utilised and extended for practical real-world portfolio management.

Answer to the Question Strong evidence from our numerical simulations and empir-
ical tests shows that the ODE-based dynamic mean-variance method has major
benefits over traditional static mean-variance distributions. First, with respect to dis-
cretisation techniques (Euler vs. RK4), the results show that although the Runge-
Kutta (RK4) integrator shows quicker convergence and less portfolio turnover than
the fundamental Euler approach, both methods converge consistently to a stable-
portfolio path. RK4 has somewhat greater processing costs; however, the resulting
time evolution of portfolio weights is usually more continuous, which reduces sud-
den changes in allocation. Therefore, in the context of market fluctuations, RK4-
based solutions appear less sensitive to significant transaction costs because of their
lower rebalancing volumes. Regarding sector-specific constraints, the application of
maximum exposure limits in some sectors or asset groups essentially reduces exces-
sively high levels of risk while maintaining a significant net benefit of the dynamic
strategy over the stationary benchmarks. The natural respect of the simplex constraint
(completely invested, non-negative weights) and the sector cap comes from the basic
mechanism: gradient-flow updates plus the projection. In fact, the extra projection
step does not significantly compromise the performance and adds very little process-
ing burden. In particular, the sector constraint results in a somewhat lower turnover

because many tiny trades aiming at the maximum are projected back into a reason-
able zone, thereby restricting the risk of significant bets on any one sector.

   When transaction cost penalties are explicitly incorporated into the mean–vari-
ance objective, the revised gradient flow model consistently reduces the rebalancing
frequency. The findings demonstrate that the temporal evolution of the ODE solution
adapts to penalise frequent trading, effectively achieving a more optimal risk–return–
cost balance than the unconstrained strategy. This effect is more pronounced under
higher penalty coefficients, such that even marginal gains from minor adjustments do
not justify the incurrence of the transaction fees. In comparison to static approaches,
the ODE-based method consistently outperforms static approaches in terms of net
returns, primarily because of the interaction between time-varying risk aversion,
explicit friction penalties, and the incremental weight adjustments of the gradient
flow. Finally, augmenting the approach with both an objective function penalty and
a maximum cumulative transaction-cost budget further refines the strategy: the cost
budget ensures that the total trading over the horizon cannot exceed a threshold,
while the cost penalty in the objective penalises trades locally on each rebalancing
step. Together, these constraints induce a notably more stable allocation path with sig-
nificantly lower aggregate turnover, although there is a modest reduction in realised
returns compared with unconstrained dynamic ODE solutions. Overall, we conclude
that imposing both constraints yields robust and operationally feasible solutions
for investment managers seeking stringent control of total trading costs across the
entire horizon. In summary, our gradient-flow-based dynamic mean–variance model,
particularly when discretised by RK4 rather than Euler, yields competitively high
returns, low realised volatility, and precisely controlled exposure across a range of
constraint scenarios. Sector constraints, transaction cost penalties, and total cost bud-
gets each serve distinct functions: mitigating over-concentrated positions, limiting
excessive trading, and enforcing overall frictional discipline. These findings elucidate
the potential of ODE-driven dynamic mean–variance approaches to surpass simpler
static or purely Markovian rebalancing strategies in practical-portfoliomanagement.

   This review aims to discuss recent developments in dynamic mean-variance port-
folio management to overcome these difficulties.

1. Robust optimisation and model risk. The major criticism of the Markow-
   itz approach is that it is very sensitive to errors in input parameters, especially
   expected returns. This can have a significant effect on the efficient frontier which
   means that the effects of estimation errors are amplified. The difficulty of estimat-
   ing expected returns, known as the ’mean blur, presents a major problem in the
   application of the Markowitz model in practice (Kuhn et al., 2009). To solve this
   issue, researchers have proposed robust portfolio optimisation techniques. These
   approaches introduce an ambiguity set consisting of several probabilistic models
   or rival probability measures. The objective is to minimise the worst-case vari-
   ance and achieve the return target constraints for each rival probability measure.
   This approach has a non-inferiority property, which guarantees that the imple-
   mented strategy results in a portfolio variance that is worse than the predicted
   variance only if the most pessimistic model is true (Kuhn et al., 2009). Several
   methods have been proposed to define ambiguity sets. Some researchers use
   finite sets of models derived from different estimation procedures. Others define
   confidence intervals for certain moments of the return distribution. Another way
   is to specify a reference measure which is most likely and then define the ambi-
   guity set as an ϵ-neighbourhood of this measure (Kuhn et al., 2009).
2. The concept of optimising while ensuring time consistency. Enhancing a port-
   folio dynamically expands the Markowitz model by incorporating time periods
   or continuous intervals to facilitate portfolio adjustments within the investment
   timeline for potential performance enhancements (Kuhn et al., 2009). Zhang
   examined the challenge of selecting a portfolio using a 3/2 stochastic volatility
   model. The researcher provides formulas for both fixed time inconsistent and
   changing time consistent approaches (Zhang, 2021). Zhangs method focuses
   on addressing time inconsistency head-on, an acknowledged challenge in mean
   variance optimisation. This strategy ensures that the wealth process remains
   consistently lower than the target until the end time. This offers insights into
   how optimal portfolios evolve over time (Zhang, 2021). van Staden et al. (2021)
   study in this paper the unexpectedly strong resilience of dynamic mean-variance
   portfolio optimisation to model misspecification errors. Additionally, the time-
   consistent mean variance (TCMV) and precommitment mean variance (PCMV)
   are considered. They find that although the two methods have differing robust-
   ness characteristics, the mean-variance results are generally resistant to model
   misspecification because the mistakes are caused by particular ratios of model

   cal solutions for measuring the effects of model misspecification on mean-vari-
   ance outcomes. They establish that when no investment constraints are in effect,
   PCMV is less robust than TCMV in modelling misspecification errors. How-
   ever, when modelling realistic investment constraints such as leverage limits,
   numerical evidence indicates that PCMV can be more robust than TCMV. Lan-

   while considering constraints and uncertainty in the model.
3. Continuous Compound Return The new method presented by Wang et al.
   emphasises growth in investments over profitability, which is more in line with
   optimising expected utility in a continuous setting linking mean variance analy-
   sis with the fractional Kelly criterion. One major advantage of this method is that
   it meets these four requirements. It can be used in multi-period situations. This
   aligns with utility theory and offers a consistent investment strategy over time.
   It establishes a clear link between risk and reward. They develop an investment
   plan that remains consistent over time and offer formulas for the efficient frontier
   based on mean variance and return adjusted for risk. This method provides inves-
   tors with a way to establish goals that match their risk tolerance.
4. Transaction Costs and Constraints Kuhn et al. include transaction costs
   and allocation limitations into their robust dynamic model. This enhancement
   improves the model’s realism by incorporating practical factors encountered by
   investors (Kuhn et al., 2009). The incorporation of transaction costs and limits in
   this approach enhances its fidelity to actual investment situations. Nonetheless, it
   also augments the model complexity, making numerical resolution more difficult
   (Kuhn et al., 2009).

6. Empirical Performance and Limitations Empirical analyses by Kuhn et al.
   (2009) indicate that robust dynamic portfolios can outperform both static and
   traditional Markowitz portfolios. Nevertheless, these findings are essentially
   derived from simulated data, with minimal evaluation of historical returns (Kuhn
   et al., 2009). Zhang presents numerical analyses demonstrating the influence
   of model parameters on the efficient frontier and the difference between stati-
   cally and dynamically optimal wealth processes. These studies provide insights
   into the behaviour of optimal portfolios under varying scenarios (Zhang, 2021).
   Zhang (2021) illustrates that their method, established on continuous compound
   return, meets the expected utility maximisation problem and creates a link to
   the fractional Kelly criterion. This relationship establishes a theoretical basis
   for the widespread use of the fractional Kelly criterion in investment practices.
   Especially when the number of assets or time periods increases, the combination
   of resilience with dynamic optimisation produces increasingly complex mod-
   els that can be computationally difficult to solve. Model definition significantly
   influences the performance of resilient dynamic models using the ambiguity set.
   While an inadequately strong set may not sufficiently guard against estimation
   errors, a conservative ambiguity set can lead to overly cautious measures. Many
   models assume the series independence of returns to preserve tractability. This
   assumption may not completely reflect the time dependencies observed in actual
   financial markets, even if it simplifies the research. Potential limitations are
   introduced by the need to approximate infinite-dimensional issues using finite-
   dimensional models. Although methods exist to minimise these mistakes, their
   relevance in useful applications remains questionable. Given the complex nature
   of these models, careful out-of-sample testing and validation are essential. How-
   ever, the long-term nature of dynamic approaches can make thorough backtest-
   ing challenging.

Thus, dynamic robust mean-variance portfolio optimisation represents a clear pro-
gression from classical portfolio theory and its shortcomings. These approaches have
the potential to provide better portfolio returns and risk management through robust-
ness to model risk and flexibility to adapt to market changes. However, these models
are more complex and computationally intensive and therefore require careful appli-
cation in real-world settings. Some possible directions for future research include
designing more efficient solution algorithms, studying other types of ambiguity set
specifications, and investigating the possibility of incorporating machine learning
artifices to improve the model’s accuracy and flexibility.
   We provide an overview of the theoretical foundations and algorithmic develop-
ments of the simplex projection method. The research starts with (Chen & Ye, 2011)
focusing on very efficient projections to the standard simplex, which reduces the

problem to one variable minimisation in a strictly convex objective sense. Improve-
ments and variations soon emerged, such as the capped simplex with upper bounds
by Wang and Lu (2015) and projection to probability simplex by Wang and Carreira-
Perpinán (2013). Simultaneously, Condat (2016) work on projecting to simplex and
ℓ1 norm ball improves the algorithm via threshold updating, whereas it refers to the
classic variable-fixing technique by Michelot. The unifying theme is that these algo-
rithms take advantage of the explicit expression of the solution and use bisection
or thresholding to achieve the speed which is desirable for large-scale optimisation
tasks.
   This method is crucial in several major fields, including spectral unmixing (Heylen
et al., 2011), where the abundance coefficients must be non-negative, and remotely
sensed imagery (Kumar et al., 2015), where feature extraction benefits from fast
projection algorithms. In addition, sophisticated optimisation strategies in machine
learning, including online or first-order methods, which use the projection of the gra-
dient descent steps onto the constrained domain (probability simplex) to avoid leav-
ing the feasible region, are crucial (Krichene et al., 2015). In addition, they applied
the technique to Bregman projections and extended it to other distances, which are
also amenable to closed-form or bisection-based solutions. In summary, the literature
shows that simplex projection is easy to describe conceptually in terms of partial sort-
ing, threshold detection, and bisection, yet it is powerful for large-scale optimisation
or data analysis pipelines, where efficiency is critical.
   Euler’s approach is a basic numerical method for ordinary differential equations
(ODEs) initial value problems, which can be traced back to Euler himself (Fadugba
et al., 2012). Hanna (1988) points out that advanced strategies are no exception, as
they are also based on this simplicity. However, many changes have been suggested
to solve the problems that the fundamental Euler method suffers from in terms of
stability and precision. These include the deep Euler technique (Shen et al., 2020),
hybrid Euler methods (Zahro et al., 2020), and an improved Euler method (Hanna,
1988). Stability, convergence, and error analyses were performed using this approach
(Shen et al., 2020; Islam, 2015a). In particular, the forward Euler technique has been
investigated for its application and efficiency in solving ODEs (Kang et al., 2022).
Euler’s method has been applied using computational tools such as SCILAB and has
been compared with other numerical approaches (Salleh, 2012). Euler’s approach
and its variants remain useful in many fields, including engineering and scientific
computations, notwithstanding their simplicity.
   The Runge-Kutta fourth-order method is one of the most popular methods for
solving ordinary differential equations, particularly for initial value problems (Islam,
2015b). Although it was initially developed for first-order ODEs, recent develop-
ments have been made regarding its application to higher order equations. Hussain
et al. (2015) and Ghawadri et al. (2019) proposed Runge-Kutta-type methods for
the direct integration of fourth-order ordinary differential equations and found that
the new methods were more accurate and efficient than the method that involved
the reduction of the fourth-order systems into two first-order systems. Adegboye et
al. (2020) suggested a fifth-order Runge-Kutta method for the direct integration of
general fourth-order ordinary differential equations based on Nyström-type methods.
Zurmühl (1948) presented a generalisation of the Runge-Kutta procedure for the inte-

2.1 Motivations, Research Gap and Contributions

Markowitz introduced his mean–variance paradigm as a static optimisation method
that uses fixed expected returns and covariance estimates to determine portfolio
weights during a single time period. The elegant yet simple model does not work well
when markets change because it does not account for changes in market conditions,
investor preferences, or trading costs. The model fails to optimise portfolio decisions
in realistic market scenarios with changing parameters and trading restrictions.
   The evolution of market conditions and trading costs has prompted researchers to
develop dynamic stochastic control models using Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman (HJB)
equations for continuous-time portfolio optimisation. The analytical complexity and
high computational costs make it challenging to solve these models when transaction
costs and state constraints need to be incorporated. The problem of dimensional-
ity growth makes it impossible to find practical solutions to the associated PDEs
when they operate in high-dimensional environments. The globally optimal poli-
cies derived from HJB-based value functions do not perform well with real trading
systems because they do not handle sharp constraint boundaries or local structural
features.

   Mean–variance optimisation methods have two branches, PCMV and TCMV
which partition the investment horizon into separate intervals. These approaches
have two main limitations: they make assumptions about steady or slowly changing
risk preferences, and they do not have sufficient mathematical power to capture how
investors change their behaviour. The portfolio weight trajectories over time remain
unclear, and the approach does not consider intraperiod portfolio adjustments.
   The Autonomous Rebalancing Portfolio Allocation (ARPA) model resolves some
of these problems through feedback-based reallocation methods. These models fail
to create a complete continuous-time optimisation system which relies on differential
equations. The ARPA models operate without a proper ODE formulation for portfolio
weight evolution, including realistic time-dependent risk aversion models, transac-

6.3.9 Economic Interpretation and Relevance

Market Environment The model assumes an investor who trades a cross–section of
N risky assets over a finite horizon [0, T]. The moment processes (µ(t), Σ(t)) are
estimated on a short rolling window, reflecting the institutional practice of valuing
recent data more highly than stale ones. Such piece-wise constant beliefs are con-
sistent with the empirical evidence that drifts and covariances are not stationary, yet
their dynamics are slow relative to the execution frequency at which asset managers
rebalance their portfolios.

Dynamic Risk Preferences Time-varying risk aversion λ(t) = λ0 + Ae−Bt is intro-
duced to mimic a common portfolio–construction paradigm in which investors
accept progressively higher risk as the horizon shortens (e.g. liability-driven pension
schemes or glidepath funds). Embedding λ(t) directly in the instantaneous mean–
variance utility (1) allows the allocation to adjust continuously to the evolving risk
budget, without ad hoc regime switches.

From Static Optimisation to Gradient Flow Re–solving the full Markowitz pro-
gramme at every date would yield piece-wise constant weights exhibiting violent
“jump” behaviour and high turnover. Instead, we embed the objective in a gradient-
flow ODE

                       ẇ(t) = kﬂow (α µ(t) − 2 λ(t) Σ(t) w(t))

which can be interpreted as the first-order condition of an investor who trades incre-
mentally towards the myopic optimum while internalising adjustment costs. The gain
parameter kﬂow thus has a clear economic meaning: it is the speed at which the man-
ager is willing to close the gap between the current portfolio and frictionless target.

Trading Frictions and Liquidity Constraints Empirical microstructure studies docu-
ment that execution cost is convex in trading volume, ranging from linear bid–ask
components to subquadratic market-impact terms. We therefore penalise trades with
a separable convex functional C(∆w) admits the four most prevalent shapes: (i)
ℓ1 (pure bid–ask), (ii) quadratic (Almgren–Chriss), (iii) a power law approximating
the square-root impact observed in block trades, and (iv) Huber, which is quadratic
for small orders but linear for large ones. Because the cost enters the discrete problem
via a proximal update, any convex specification can be accommodated without sacri-
ficing computational efficiency or closed-form tractability, respectively.

Long-only, Fully Invested Mandate Many mutual funds, pension plans, and ETFs are
prohibited from short sales or leverage. We encode this mandate through the simplex

Numerical Scheme and Implementation Choice The forward–Euler and fourth-

Managerial Implications By varying the cost parameters (κ, γ, p, δ) and the speed
kﬂow , a practitioner can trace an empirical efficient frontier of realised return versus
trading expense, quantify the marginal value of capacity, and stress-test how tighter
budget rules compress risk-adjusted performance. Because the allocation path is con-
tinuous in inputs, the model is also well suited to scenario analysis under stressed
moments (µ, Σ), an increasingly important feature in regulatory stress testing and
climate risk contexts.

Position in the Literature The formulation marries the classical Markowitz paradigm
to modern execution cost theory and proximal optimisation methods. It bridges the
gap between multi-period mean–variance control (which is often intractable outside
quadratic cost) and single-period portfolio selection (which ignores path-dependent
trading frictions), providing a computationally light yet economically rich frame-
work that can be calibrated on high-frequency execution data and rolled out in large-
scale asset-allocation systems.

6.3.12 Future Extension: Non-Linear Transaction Costs

This study implements a quadratic transaction cost model because it matches the convex
smooth characteristics of continuous-time portfolio optimisation and works well with
ordinary differential equation (ODE) frameworks. The quadratic formulation enables
efficient trading activity penalties through analytical methods within a mathematically
tractable framework.
   The actual transaction costs that institutional investors face in practice demon-
strate non-linear behaviour because of market impact effects, limited liquidity, and
large trade sizes. The market microstructure literature demonstrates that realistic
transaction costs follow power-law distributions and exhibit linear bid-ask spreads
and hybrid behaviours which transition from quadratic to linear penalties at different
trade sizes. Accurate modelling of complex transaction cost structures remains essen-
tial for representing actual execution conditions in high-volume institutional trading.
   The present ODE-based optimisation framework can be conceptually extended to
include nonlinear transaction cost structures, but it presents significant implemen-
tation difficulties. The modification process requires changes to both the objective

tions. The management of the resulting non-smooth dynamics requires sophisticated
optimisation methodologies, including subgradient techniques, variational inequality

   This development makes the model more realistic for institutional investors, which
increases both the mathematical and computational requirements. The optimisation
results would achieve higher economic authenticity through the implementation of
realistic nonlinear transaction costs, especially when liquidity constraints and market
impacts are significant.
   The current research focuses on developing and validating dynamic mean-vari-
ance optimisation with quadratic transaction costs; therefore, the authors propose
non-linear cost extensions as future research directions for computational implemen-
tation and rigorous exploration.

7 Conclusion
Summary This study suggests ODE-based dynamic mean variance optimisation as a
new approach to conventional static mean variance portfolio optimisation. The gra-
dient flow ODE-based formulation of the portfolio weight evolution enables con-
tinuous time dynamic adjustments of the weights, along with the incorporation of
practical investment constraints. We used Euler and Runge–Kutta (RK4) numerical
integrations to discretise the model and used it for practical application in portfolio
optimisation. Four incremental extensions of the ODE framework were systemati-
cally studied: (i) simplex constraint enforcement, (ii) sector constraints, (iii) transac-
tion cost penalties in the objective function, and (iv) total transaction cost budget
constraints. We found that the ODE-based approach is better than the static mean-



13
An ODE-Based Dynamic Mean-Variance Portfolio Optimisation with…


variance models in terms of controlling risk, limiting transaction costs, and avoid-
ing overconcentration in certain sectors. Moreover, we noticed that RK4 integration
generates smoother allocation paths than the Euler updates and is thus a better choice
whenever computational expense is not a concern.

Limitations Our approach has several limitations. First, the ODE formulation is of
portfolio evolution in continuous time, while rebalancing occurs at discrete points in
time and is affected by execution risks. Market impact is not fully incorporated into
the model, especially when trading in illiquid markets, which can have a major effect
on realised trading costs. Second, our approach is based on a fixed time-varying risk
aversion level λ(t) and transaction cost parameters κ, which may be difficult to quan-
tify or to empirically support. In practice, adaptive risk aversion that dynamically
changes with the market may be more relevant to investors. Third, while the proximal
ℓ1 method helps regularise transactions, it does not provide a direct calculation of the
trade execution strategy with respect to price impact. This may result in suboptimal
performance in actual trading, particularly for high-frequency or large-scale institu-
tional trading.

Future Work There are several possible directions for extending the present research.
First, an optimal adaptive risk aversion function λ(t) can be incorporated according to
real-time market dynamics or investor preferences, which can be achieved using rein-
forcement learning or Bayesian filtering. Second, a more realistic implementation for
institutional investors could be an extended mean-variance formulation incorporating
market frictions, such as price slippage and liquidity constraints. Our approach can
also be compared with dynamic rebalancing strategies across different markets (e.g.
equities, bonds, and commodities) when extended to multi-asset classes. A promising
direction is to combine stochastic control techniques, such as solving the HJB equa-
tion, to obtain fully time-consistent solutions to the problem. Finally, to justify the
practicality of the ODE-based method, the efficiency of the method must be tested
using real trading data, and transaction cost penalties and sector constraints must be
refined for actual investment scenarios. To address these limitations and investigate
these extensions, future work can be used to elaborate ODE-based portfolio optimi-
sation as a more elaborate and more applicable in practice than the traditional mean-
variance model approach.

Important Extension to the Work Building on the work presented, a potential exten-
sion of the ODE-based dynamic mean variance model is its use as a feature extrac-
tion tool in portfolio optimisation and financial modelling. Most prior studies have
used statistical-factor models. Such as principal component analysis, Fama-French
factors, or machine learning-based feature selection to build portfolios. However,
these methods do not capture the dynamics of portfolio weights and their impact on
rebalancing. Although the ODE framework captures the velocity of weight changes,
it does not capture the effects of sector constraints and transaction cost penalties over
time. Thus, the dynamics of the system can be studied to extract meaningful financial
signals that capture market sentiment, trading behaviour, and portfolio volatility.

                                                                                           M. H. Alkhudaydi

    One way of extracting features is to look at the velocity of portfolio weight adjust-
ments as the gradient dw(t)dt   from the ODE system. This velocity feature provides a
direct measure of trading activity momentum: large absolute values correspond to fre-
quent rebalancing, whereas low values correspond to a situation of strategic equilibrium.
Another important feature is provided by the proximal ℓ1 step, which implements soft
thresholding of weight modifications to minimise the number of transactions. The inten-
sity of this shrinkage informs us of how far the optimisation process penalises large
trades and distinguishes between small and large turnover trading patterns. Furthermore,
the sector constraint activation frequency, which indicates the frequency with which the
portfolio exceeds a threshold, can be used to measure market diversification pressure.
When combined, these features can be used to develop a data-driven model of portfolio
rebalancing dynamics that can be fed into machine learning models, such as reinforce-
ment learning for trading strategies or clustering algorithms for market regime detection.
    Several directions are available for future studies. First, it is worth investigating how
to incorporate the SDE into the ODE system to learn the feature dynamics in the pres-
ence of market noise. Second, we suggest examining the combination of ODE features
with classical factor models to determine whether the dynamics of rebalancing are more
informative than static risk factors. In addition, time-series clustering algorithms can be
applied to the ODE features to discover underlying trading markets and their dynamics,
which can help gain insights into the structure of the market and the adaptiveness of
portfolios. Thus, the proposed ODE framework can be seen as a way of extending the
concepts of traditional portfolio optimisation and AI-enabled asset management across
the spectrum of quantitative finance and investment decision-making and for beating
benchmark returns by effectively controlling risks. The ODE-based dynamic mean-
variance framework requires future research to compare its performance with stochas-
tic control models, dynamic programming, and reinforcement learning-based portfolio
strategies. These methods provide different approaches to manage uncertainty, discrete
decision stages, and model-free learning; however, they typically require more complex
computations and do not produce smooth control trajectories. A thorough comparative
analysis of these dynamic optimisation paradigms would reveal important trade-offs
between their tractability, interpretability, and adaptability.
